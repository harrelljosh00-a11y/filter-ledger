**Short version:** the number on the box tells you *what kind* of claim was certified. The certification listing tells you *which contaminants* were certified. You need both.

## Why certifications matter at all

There's no federal approval process for home water filters. In the US, residential filters, purifiers and reverse osmosis systems aren't federally regulated for performance. Instead, independent bodies certify products against voluntary national standards written through NSF and ANSI. Certification means an independent body checked the product's materials, structure and specific reduction claims.

One thing trips people up: **the numbers aren't a ranking.** NSF says the numbers simply reflect the order in which the standards were written. A "58" isn't "better" than a "53". They cover different things.

## The four standards you'll see most

| Standard | What it covers | Typical claims | Who should care |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NSF/ANSI 42** | *Aesthetic* effects: things that affect taste, smell and appearance | Chlorine, taste and odor, particulates | Anyone who mainly dislikes how tap water tastes |
| **NSF/ANSI 53** | *Health-related* contaminants | Examples include lead, cysts like *Cryptosporidium*, and VOCs (claims vary by product) | Anyone with a specific health-related contaminant in their water report |
| **NSF/ANSI 58** | *Reverse osmosis* systems | Total dissolved solids (TDS) reduction, plus optional specific contaminant claims | People considering an RO system |
| **NSF/ANSI 401** | *Emerging compounds / incidental contaminants*: substances found at trace levels | Examples include certain prescription and over-the-counter drug residues, herbicides and pesticides | People who want an extra margin beyond the regulated list |

Each of these standards also covers **material safety** (the filter itself shouldn't add contaminants, including lead content limits under NSF/ANSI/CAN 372) and **structural integrity**.

## The catch: certification is per contaminant

This is the most important line on this page. A product isn't "NSF 53 certified" in general. It's certified to NSF/ANSI 53 **for specific contaminants**.

For example, NSF/ANSI 42 covers several contaminants (chlorine, chloramine, iron and others). A filter can be certified under 42 **for chlorine only**, which tells you nothing about chloramine. So if your city uses chloramine, "NSF 42 certified" on the box isn't enough. You need chloramine on the listing.

**Rule of thumb:** find the contaminant you care about on the official listing, next to the model number you're buying.

## "Certified" vs "tested to" vs "meets"

You'll see three kinds of wording:

1. **"Certified by NSF / WQA / IAPMO to NSF/ANSI 53 for lead."** An accredited certifier tested the product and keeps checking it over time. This is the strongest claim. {{verdict:certified}}
2. **"Tested to NSF/ANSI 53 standards by an independent lab."** A lab ran the standard's test method, usually once, but nobody certified the product. That's useful evidence, but weaker. {{verdict:lab-tested}}
3. **"Meets NSF standards" or "removes 99% of contaminants"** with no lab or certifier named. That's marketing, and you can't verify it. {{verdict:claim-only}}

At Filter Ledger, every product claim gets one of those three labels, and certified claims always link to the listing.

## How to check any filter in about a minute

1. Find the **exact model number** (and the replacement cartridge model; it matters).
2. Search the certifier's public database:
   - NSF: [info.nsf.org/Certified/dwtu](https://info.nsf.org/Certified/dwtu/)
   - WQA: [find.wqa.org](https://find.wqa.org/find-products)
   - IAPMO: [pld.iapmo.org](https://pld.iapmo.org/)
3. Open the listing and check that **your contaminant** appears next to **that model**.
4. If the brand says "certified" but you can't find a listing, treat the claim as unverified until the brand shows you one.

## Where to start if you don't know what's in your water

NSF's own advice is to find out what's in your water first. If you're on city water, read your utility's annual water quality report, often called a Consumer Confidence Report. If you have a private well, test with a certified lab. Then buy a filter certified for what you actually found.

## A note on PFAS (2026)

In 2024, EPA set enforceable limits of 4 parts per trillion for two PFAS, PFOA and PFOS. In May 2026, EPA proposed keeping those two limits while letting utilities apply for more time to comply (to 2031 instead of 2029), and rescinding limits for four other PFAS. If PFAS is your concern, look for **PFOA/PFOS named on the certification listing itself**, not just "PFAS" on the packaging.
